# Uncodixfy

Make the interface feel reliable, coherent, and specific to its purpose. Default to orderly structure, sufficient information, consistent components, and measured visual character. A restrained design and an expressive design can both succeed. Do not replace one generic style with another.

## Start with the purpose

Identify what the visitor should understand or do, the kind of page being made, and the strongest available content or visual assets. Let those determine hierarchy and visual tone.

- Tools and administrative screens usually benefit from compact navigation, predictable controls, and efficient scanning.
- Character pages, portfolios, and cultural or editorial sites can benefit from expressive typography, prominent imagery, and a memorable composition.
- Marketing pages need a clear offer and credible evidence. A dashboard structure is not a universal layout.

These are starting points, not templates. Respect the user's direction and any existing design system. Do not expand a small page into a full product to demonstrate a style.

## Establish a stable system

Use a repeatable grid and shared alignment anchors for titles, content, controls, and section boundaries. Define a small consistent scale for typography, spacing, control heights, borders, and corner treatments. Related elements should behave and look related. Fix weak hierarchy and inconsistent alignment before adding effects.

For operational software, organize around the working sequence: locate records, inspect fields, act, and verify the result. Keep search, list toolbar, table, and pagination in predictable positions. Aim for useful desktop density so users can compare records without decorative headers or oversized gaps. Let long names wrap or truncate deliberately without crowding actions. Align money and quantities consistently; keep units, dates, selection, empty results, and disabled actions unambiguous.

Distinguish navigation, work surface, and overlays with a small number of clear levels. Avoid nested shells, repeated card containers, or oversized metrics that push working content below the fold. Use summaries only when they help the task and derive them from actual data. Offer a clear primary action, with related secondary actions nearby. Preserve predictable keyboard focus and a clear way out of dialogs.

Visual personality should coexist with this structure. A recognizable product does not require a spectacular hero, and a stable interface does not require every component to be a sharp rectangle. On expressive public pages, retain grid relationships and component consistency while giving the subject more space. Do not impose ERP density or navigation on unrelated page types.

## Build a coherent identity

Draw colors and typography from the supplied brand, subject, imagery, or references before consulting a generic palette. Choose readable supporting colors and verify contrast in context. Do not default to either dark mode or a muted neutral scheme.

Give the main content or visual asset an intentional role in the composition. Select related visual choices and carry them through the page. Typography, color, geometry, image framing, overlap, and motion are ordinary design materials.

Choose geometry for the subject and composition. Rectangles are not a safer or more professional default than circles, curves, arches, cutouts, rounded forms, or irregular silhouettes. Nonrectangular forms do not need stronger justification than rectangular panels. Visual balance, framing, rhythm, and personality are legitimate purposes even when a shape communicates no literal fact. Do not add explanatory labels to justify a visual choice.

Judge the result as a composition: are its forms coherent, is the content clear, and does the page suit its subject? Correct competing motifs, confusing boundaries, poor image crops, or effects that obscure content. Do not resolve every issue by flattening the design into rectangular blocks. Do not require a quota of curves, decorative elements, or other shapes either.

## Avoid interchangeable structure

Choose sections because the content needs them. Do not automatically produce an eyebrow label, slogan, two calls to action, three feature cards, statistics, and a decorative footer.

Use tables for comparable facts, lists for sequences, and cards when items need independent boundaries or actions. Do not enclose every paragraph in a card. Write concrete copy about the actual subject; avoid filler, invented claims, and labels that only explain the layout.

Keep navigation and controls familiar enough to use without explanation. Use space to establish relationships, not to inflate a short page. Preserve the most important subject matter when adapting the layout to mobile; reconsider scale and ordering rather than simply stacking the desktop design.

## Preserve the substance

Removing low-value labels and repeated explanations is a copy edit, not an instruction to summarize the entire page. Preserve the requested facts, distinct ideas, narrative depth, and useful sections. Do not automatically merge sections, shorten the body, or turn a full introduction into a profile summary to appear clean. When reflowing the layout, preserve access to the content. If source material is sparse, do not invent facts or repeat information merely to fill space.

## Give supporting text a job

Use visible text for subject matter, orientation, actions, or information the visitor actually needs. Do not add words merely to make a composition look designed.

- Omit decorative category labels, section numbers, English translations of an adjacent heading, image-side metadata, and pre-heading microcopy unless they supply distinct information or belong to an established design system. Labels such as “CHARACTER / NAME”, “ABOUT NAME”, or “01 / EXPLORE” are not a default component.
- Do not explain a self-evident section, announce that a page is an introduction, or describe the interface's own design and implementation. Avoid helper sentences beneath every heading and decorative slogans above ordinary content.
- State each fact where it is most useful. Do not repeat the same identity or attribute in a header subtitle, portrait caption, badge, introduction, and fact table. Repeat only when a real navigation, comprehension, or accessibility need justifies it.
- Prefer a meaningful heading or the content itself over a heading plus an ornamental label plus a sentence introducing the content. Do not replace removed filler with a different generic phrase or enlarge the empty space it leaves.
- Keep necessary labels, disambiguating qualifiers, useful image captions, error and form guidance, dates, and requested attribution. A real alternate name can be content; an English label that merely restates the nearby section title is decoration. Small type is available for genuinely secondary information.
- Default to no disclaimer or self-description. Do not add “unofficial introduction”, “for demonstration only”, “fan-made”, “generated by AI”, “responsive design”, or similar footer text simply because it is true or seems prudent. Truth alone does not make a sentence useful. Include such notices only for a concrete applicable requirement or a current explicit user request; do not infer that they are required from the page's subject. Follow the user's later instruction to remove them rather than carrying forward stale boilerplate.
- Keep requested source credits as concise links. A useful source link does not justify a neighboring disclaimer or an explanatory paragraph. Do not append a footer merely to make the page look complete. Put implementation notes and delivery caveats in the chat, not in the product.

Before finishing, apply the deletion test to every label, caption, subtitle, and helper sentence: if removing it loses no needed fact, orientation, action guidance, or required attribution, remove it. Preserve personality through the actual subject matter, composition, imagery, and typography.

## Check the result

Inspect the rendered interface at desktop and mobile sizes when tools permit. Check legibility, image framing, content order, keyboard focus, links, and overflow. Respect reduced-motion preferences when adding movement.

Ask whether the design choices suit this audience and content, whether the page has a clear focal point, and whether the visual system remains consistent. If the same composition could introduce an unrelated product after swapping the title and accent color, revise its relationship to the content. Evaluate expressive and restrained choices on the same basis; neither minimalism nor visual complexity is a goal by itself.

Report material verification limits honestly. Apply this guidance within the requested scope and available tools.
